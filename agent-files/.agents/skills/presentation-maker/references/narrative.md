# Narrative: making the message land

## THE INVESTOR NARRATIVE RULE

Every investor deck must answer 3 questions within the first 3 slides:

1. **What is the prize?** A market-size number, set huge (slide 2).
2. **What is broken about the world today?** One sentence naming the problem.
3. **Why is this company the answer?** One sentence completing the thought.

**The formula:** S1 Cover → S2 Big Statement (prize + problem + solution in one slide) → S3 Company facts.

If an investor can't state your market, your problem, and your wedge after slide 3, the opening failed.

---

## NARRATIVE ARC — Energy management

The deck must have an energy *curve*, not a flatline. No two consecutive slides at the same intensity. Alternate dark/dense with light/sparse throughout.

**Pattern:**
Cover (high) → Big Statement (peak) → Company (grounding) → Section divider (reset) → Stats (rising) → Product (light contrast break) → Value (dark) → Team (human) → Closing (highest).

Think of it as music: you can't sustain fortissimo for 12 slides. The quiet slides are what make the loud ones land.

---

## TYPOGRAPHY AS NARRATIVE INSTRUMENT

Four techniques that make type carry the story:

1. **Ghost typography** — Place a key number at 300-340pt in a color matching the background. Invisible as text but felt as visual weight and depth.
2. **Architecture number** — A giant number (180-220pt) in a slide corner that IS both content and layout structure at once (e.g. the section number on a divider).
3. **Pre-title eyebrow** — A small ALL CAPS line with wide character spacing (4-5pt) above the headline. Sets context as a whisper before the shout.
4. **Compressed tagline** — One line at the bottom of the cover slide summarizing the entire value prop. Example: "25 years. 6 continents. One trusted partner."

## INTERNAL / STRATEGY DECK ARC

Not every deck pitches investors. Internal strategy, planning, and partner decks (e.g. SentinelCore Cyber Security planning; APS Polymers v6) follow a different arc — they build *trust and alignment*, not FOMO.

**Arc:** Cover → Big statement (the mission/stance) → **Philosophy / "Our Approach"** (how we think) → What we do → **Numbered priorities** (what we deliver) → Proof (platform/device reality) → **Quote** (conviction) → Team / human → Close.

The distinguishing beats vs. an investor deck: a **philosophy slide early** (trust before credentials) and a **conviction quote late** (instead of a hard "ask").

### Philosophy slide doctrine
Place a "how we think" slide near the front (slide 3-4). One guiding principle, stated large (~26pt), with an eyebrow label and an abstract contemplative element (texture-oval — not a photo, icon, or chart). It earns trust faster than any list of credentials because it signals *worldview*, not *résumé*.

Example principle: *"We do not move product. We move markets. Every relationship we build is engineered to outlast any single transaction."*

### Numbered priorities over bullets
When listing what you deliver / your pillars, never use a bullet list. Use a 2×2 grid of **numbered priority pills** (01-04). Numbering implies deliberate ranking and structure; bullets imply an unordered dump. The same content reads as far more credible.

### Quote slide approaches
A conviction quote can carry a whole slide with **no portrait** — quote at ~34pt, eyebrow above, attribution below, subtle low-opacity texture for depth. Use this when the *idea* matters more than *who said it*. Reserve portrait-paired quotes for customer testimonials where the person's identity is the proof.

### Strategy energy curve
Strategy decks still need an energy curve, but it's a *steadier* one than an investor pitch — trust is built by consistency, not spikes. Pattern: Cover (warm-high) → Mission statement (peak) → Philosophy (reflective dip, deliberate) → What we do (rising) → Numbered priorities (structured plateau) → Platform proof (concrete lift) → Quote (emotional peak) → Close (resolved, restrained). Still alternate dark/dense with light/sparse — just avoid the hard FOMO spike an investor deck uses.



A deck is not a document. It's a sequence of beats that should leave the audience with a specific takeaway. Before you place a single shape, decide what that takeaway is and how the deck earns it.

## The two-sentence test

Before outlining, write down — out loud, to the user, in plain words:

1. *After viewing this deck, the audience should think/believe/do: …*
2. *The single most important thing they should remember is: …*

If you can't fill in those sentences, neither the user nor the deck has a goal. Keep asking until they're sharp. A vague answer ("understand the project") produces a vague deck. A sharp answer ("approve the migration to the new architecture by end of Q2") produces a deck with a spine.

## Read-on-your-own vs. talk-along — pick one

These are different products. Mixing them produces decks that are too dense to present and too sparse to email.

- **Talk-along** (you're presenting live): few words per slide, big visuals, the slide *prompts* — you deliver the content. Speaker notes carry the script.
- **Read-on-your-own** (sent over email/Slack, board pre-reads, decks shared async): each slide is self-contained. More text, more annotation, the slide *is* the content.

Ask the user which. If it's both, build for read-on-your-own and tell them to summarize verbally while presenting — never the reverse.

## Narrative arcs that work

Pick one and let it shape the section order. Don't blend arcs in one deck — it dilutes the spine.

### Problem → Solution → Proof → Ask
The pitch-deck classic. Works for sales, internal proposals, fundraising.
1. The world today (problem, with stakes — *why this hurts*).
2. The shift (a trend, technology, or insight that makes the old way wrong).
3. Our solution (concrete, in one sentence on one slide before details).
4. Proof (traction, testimonials, demo, results).
5. The ask (money, headcount, approval, sign-off — *named explicitly*).

### Situation → Complication → Question → Answer
McKinsey-style consulting. Works for recommendations, strategy reviews, decisions.
1. What's happening (uncontested facts).
2. What changed or what's unstable (the complication that demands a response).
3. The question we need to answer.
4. Our answer, with reasoning.

### Before → After → Bridge
Works for change announcements, product launches, migrations.
1. Where we are.
2. Where we'll be.
3. How we get from one to the other (the plan).

### Chronological / Roadmap
Works for status updates, retrospectives, plans-on-a-page. Be careful: it's the laziest arc and easy to over-rely on. Only use when *time itself* is the structuring principle.

### Compare / Contrast
Works when the deck's job is to choose between options. One slide per option using *identical* layouts, then a decision slide that picks one and says why.

## Signposting

Long decks need landmarks. The audience needs to know where they are.

- **Section dividers**: full-bleed slides with just a section title and number ("2 / 4 · How we built it"). They reset attention.
- **Recurring tracker** (optional): a tiny bar or breadcrumb at the top of each slide showing which section you're in. Use sparingly — easy to overdo.
- **Recap slides** every 5-7 content slides in long decks: "So far we've covered X, Y; next we'll cover Z."

## Claim titles, not label titles

This is the single highest-leverage change you can make.

| Label title (avoid for content slides) | Claim title (use) |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Revenue grew 4× after we shipped self-serve |
| Architecture | We replaced 7 services with 2 — and dropped p99 by half |
| Roadmap | We can ship the new editor in Q3 if we hire two engineers in May |
| User Research | Users abandon at the credit-card step — fixing it unlocks ~$1.2M |

Claim titles let the audience grasp the message in 2 seconds and treat the slide body as evidence. Label titles force them to read everything and *infer* a claim — most audiences won't.

Reserve label titles for section dividers, agenda slides, and the cover. Everywhere else, use a claim.

## One idea per slide

If you can't summarize the slide in a single sentence, split it. Two ideas crammed together steal attention from each other. The exception is *intentional contrast layouts* (before/after, this/that, option A vs B) — those are one idea expressed as a comparison.

If the user pushes back ("I need everything on one slide for the printout"), build the slide for the presentation, and offer to generate an appendix slide that crams the details for the printout. Don't compromise the live deck.

## The "so what" rule

For every data slide, every chart, every diagram: write the *so what* directly on the slide. Don't make the audience derive it.

> Chart shows time-to-first-purchase dropping from 9 days to 3. The label under it says: **"Onboarding is no longer the bottleneck — pricing is."**

The chart is the evidence. The sentence is the point. Both belong on the slide.

## Density calibration

Rough rule of thumb for body text per slide (excluding titles and chart labels):

| Slide type | Word budget |
|---|---|
| Cover, section divider | 0–10 |
| Talk-along content slide | 15–40 |
| Read-on-your-own content slide | 60–120 |
| Appendix / reference slide | up to 200 |

If you're over the budget, you have a *split this slide* problem, not a *make the font smaller* problem.

## Endings

Don't end on "Thank you" or "Questions?" — those slides waste your most attention-loaded moment. End on:

- **The ask** (if the deck has one): the specific decision, signoff, or action you want.
- **The single sentence** you want them to remember.
- **The next step**: "We'll send the proposal Friday. Decision needed by the 14th."

A strong final slide doubles the chance the audience does what you wanted.

## Common narrative failures to watch for

- **The deck has no spine.** It's a tour of topics, not an argument. Fix: pick an arc.
- **The cover slide promises one thing, the body delivers another.** Fix: rewrite the cover title last, after the deck exists.
- **Section weights don't match section importance.** A 1-slide "problem" and a 12-slide "feature list" inverts the narrative. Fix: rebalance.
- **The deck buries the lede.** The most important slide is on slide 14. Fix: move it forward, or restructure so the build *earns* a punchline that lands earlier.
- **Every slide is the same density.** Audience attention flattens. Fix: alternate dense and sparse — a heavy slide followed by a one-line slide hits harder than two medium slides.
